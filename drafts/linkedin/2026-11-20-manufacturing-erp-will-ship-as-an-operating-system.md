Manufacturing ERP already reaches into machines, schedules the floor and can stop a line. At that point it is doing an operating system's job, whatever the sales deck calls it.

Steam spent years as an app on someone else's operating system, then Valve shipped the operating system. Three choices carried over: a compatibility layer so the old library still ran, A/B updates that fall back instead of bricking the device, and one OS shipped by more than one hardware maker.

Most of the parts for a plant version are already open: OPC UA for machine communication, soft PLC runtimes on Linux, open-source ERP cores, import tools for legacy data. The gap is the assembly, and upgrades with a tested rollback. In accounting terms, a reversing entry for software.

The essay also says where the analogy bends. SteamOS is not fully open, the ERP stays out of the control loop, and a distribution needs a maintainer with a business model.

One thing to try before your next ERP decision: list every place your ERP already acts like an operating system. That list is the real scope of the project.

Worth reading if you are weighing a plant ERP choice.
