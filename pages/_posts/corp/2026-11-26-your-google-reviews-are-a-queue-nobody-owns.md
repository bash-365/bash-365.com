In BrightLocal's 2026 surveys, 89% of US consumers expect a business owner to answer reviews and a third want the answer by the next day; about half of small-business owners say they answer at all. The gap between those two numbers is a queue on your Google Business Profile that every new customer reads and nobody on your staff owns.

## What an unanswered review costs

The expectation is now explicit. In [BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) of 1,002 US adults (a survey by a company that sells review software), 89% expect owners to respond, 32% want a response by the following day and 81% within a week. Eighty percent say they're likely to use a business that responds to all of its reviews; 42% say they're unlikely to use one that never replies.

The rating sets whether you're considered at all. In the same survey, 68% will only use a business with four stars or more, up from 55% a year earlier.

For restaurants, the rating shows up in the till. Michael Luca's [study of every Seattle restaurant from 2003 to 2009](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/12-016_a7e4a5a2-03f9-490d-b093-8f951238dba2.pdf) found that a one-star increase on Yelp leads to a 5–9% increase in revenue for independent restaurants, and no measurable effect for chains. [Anderson and Magruder](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/122/563/957-989/5079476) found that an extra half-star on Yelp makes a restaurant sell out its reservations 19 percentage points more often.

Google says the same about visibility. Its [local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) says "more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking," and that "positive reviews and helpful replies can help your business stand out."

One thing the evidence does not say: that a reply by itself raises revenue. The clearest studies of replying are on hotels. [Hotels that started replying](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v36y2017i5p645-665.html) saw ratings rise by about a tenth of a star and 12% more reviews, with negative reviews that were fewer but longer; [another study](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v37y2018i5p688-709.html) found replies drew more negative reviewing. The case for answering every review is what your customers now expect and what they read under each complaint, not a promised star.

## Why the queue goes unowned

A restaurant shows the problem most clearly. Reviews arrive during the lunch rush, during dinner service, and after close, when the person who could answer is on the line or counting the drawer. The reviews that most need an answer are the hardest to write calmly. And the reply that does damage in a restaurant is specific:

- telling a guest a dish is safe for their allergy;
- promising a refund or a free meal nobody approved;
- blaming the delivery driver, in public, for a cold order;
- repeating the phone number a guest posted in their review.

The work is real, so it slips, even in the trade that tries hardest. In [BrightLocal's 2026 survey of small-business owners](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/smb-report-enough-reviews/) (also vendor-run), 60% of food and drink businesses said they respond to reviews, against 51% of all small businesses: four in ten restaurants still leave the queue to itself.

## Treat it as a control, not a chore

Finance already solved this shape of problem. A payment run has a preparer and an approver, a policy that says what is allowed, and a record of who did what. Replying to reviews needs the same four parts:

- **The policy is your reply guidance**, written once: who signs off, what number to give, what a reply never says.
- **The preparer is the draft.** Artificial intelligence (AI) is good at the first draft: it reads the review, names what it's about, and writes a reply that follows the guidance.
- **The approver is a person** who reads the exact text and says yes, or edits it and says yes. Change a word after approval and it needs approving again.
- **The record is the queue itself**: what is waiting, how long it has waited, and what went out.

The person is not a formality. In BrightLocal's consumer survey, templated or generic responses make 50% of consumers unlikely to choose a business, and Google's own [reply tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en) say to "address the reviewer by name and acknowledge their specific feedback." A draft that sounds like everyone else's reply is worse than a short one in your own words.

## Write your reply guidance first

Whatever tool you use, or none, start with the guidance. This is a working version for a restaurant; change the bracketed parts:

```text
Speak as [restaurant name]: warm, plain and brief, like the person at the counter.
Thank the guest by first name and answer what they actually said, in two to four sentences, under 80 words.
For a problem, apologize once, never argue, and invite them to call [phone] and ask for the manager.
Never promise a refund, a discount, free food or a comp.
Never blame a staff member or a delivery app by name.
Never say a dish is safe for an allergy and never discuss a guest's health; invite them to call so the manager can go through the ingredients.
Never repeat a phone number, address or other personal detail a guest wrote.
Reply in the review's language, approved only by someone who reads it.
Sign off as "[sign-off]".
```

For other businesses, one or two lines change. A contractor takes invoice disputes to a phone call. A clinic never confirms that the reviewer is a patient: the federal health-privacy regulator settled with a [Dallas dental practice for $10,000 in 2019](https://www.hhs.gov/about/news/2019/10/02/dental-practice-pays-10000-settle-social-media-disclosures-of-patients-phi.html) and a [New Jersey psychiatric practice for $30,000 in 2023](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/manasa/index.html), both over replies to online reviews.

## The free option, and when it's enough

Google now drafts replies itself. Since [June 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/gemini-app/gemini-features-for-businesses/), the [Gemini web app](https://support.google.com/business/answer/17142585?hl=en) can draft a reply to a Business Profile review for owners who manage a single verified profile from a personal Google account. If that's you, and you answer reviews yourself, start there with the guidance above.

It stops fitting when the business does. Gemini is not available to anyone who manages more than one verified profile or signs in with a work account, and Google documents no approval step, no second approver and no record of what went out. That's the point where a system earns its keep: more than one location, a manager who answers while the owner approves, reply guidance that has to hold every time, or reviews in more than one language.

## Watch-outs

1. **Never pay for reviews or ask only happy customers.** Google [prohibits](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) incentives and selectively soliciting positive reviews. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) [rule on consumer reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), in effect since October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews, AI-generated ones included, and paying for reviews that express a particular sentiment; the FTC [warns](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) that asking only happy customers "could violate the FTC Act." Asking every customer, with no incentive, is fine.
2. **Keep a person on every reply.** Google's [policy for software that uses its Business Profile application programming interfaces (APIs)](https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/policies) forbids triggering review replies "without the user's prior specific and express consent"; approving each reply is the simplest way to meet it. A tool that auto-posts five-star replies is a setting away from posting a bad one.
3. **Plan for Google's approval and its storage rule.** Google approves each software project before it can reach reviews, and the applicant must manage a profile verified and active for 60 days or more, with a website listed on it ([prerequisites](https://developers.google.com/my-business/content/prereqs)). The same policy limits how long software may keep review content to 30 days and forbids aggregating it, so ask any vendor how they meet it.
